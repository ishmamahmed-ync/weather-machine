[Slide 1 : The Gaps in the Weather Machine]
Title: The Gaps in the Weather Machine
Description: For most of history, people guessed the weather. In the last fifty years, recorded disasters have gone up almost five times. But deaths have gone down almost three times, because of early warnings. To warn people, we first need to see the storm coming. That depends on a huge system of instruments around the world.

[Slide 2 : The weather machine]
Title: The weather machine
Description: Every forecast uses millions of measurements from all over the world. Together, these instruments make up the weather machine. It only works because countries share their data. Even the US receives three times more weather data than it sends out. No country can forecast on its own.

[Slide 6 : The Gaps]
Title: The Gaps
Description: The US has 78,567 weather stations. That's 59% of all the stations on Earth. Africa is more than three times bigger and has 2,119. The US and Europe have 636 weather radars for 1.1 billion people. Africa has 37 for 1.2 billion.

[Slide 7 : The Gaps]
Title: The Gaps
Description: The ocean has gaps too. Argo floats drift with the currents, so they miss coastlines, closed-in seas and ice. One of these gaps is off the coast of Mozambique. The Mozambique Channel gets a third of the average number of float readings. In March 2019, a cyclone grew stronger there.

[Slide 8 : The Gaps: Cell by cell]
Title: The Gaps: Cell by cell
Description: The WMO says flat land needs about one rain gauge for every 575 km². That's around 21 gauges in each one-degree square. Tap a square to see how it compares. Only 13.4% of the world's land meets this standard. Europe has 2.4 gauges per 1,000 km². Africa has 0.09.

[Slide 10 : Mozambique, 2019]
Title: Mozambique, 2019
Description: Mozambique is one of the poorest countries in the world, and one of the most exposed to climate change. More than 60% of its people live in low-lying areas along the coast. Only 19 of its weather stations send data to the global archive. And much of the rain that floods its rivers falls in neighbouring countries.

[Slide 11 : One season, three storms]
Title: One season, three storms
Description: 21 January 2019: Tropical Storm Desmond causes severe flooding. 52 days later, Cyclone Idai hits. 42 days after that, Cyclone Kenneth. Mozambique had never been hit by this many cyclones in one season. For people there, climate change was no longer abstract.

[Slide 12 : The storm was expected. The flood was not.]
Title: The storm was expected. The flood was not.
Description: Forecasters saw Idai coming five days before it hit, and warnings went out. But no one saw the flood coming. Idai moved inland and dropped heavy rain over Zimbabwe, and the rivers carried that water back into Mozambique. The plain around the Pungwe and Buzi rivers turned into an inland sea. Satellites can track a storm. But to know where its rain will go, you need rain gauges on the ground.

[Slide 13 : Before and after]
Title: Before and after

[Slide 14 : Idai in numbers]
Title: Idai in numbers
Description: 1.85 million people affected. 603 people died in Mozambique, and more than 1,000 across three countries. 122,700 houses destroyed and 111,200 damaged. 77 health centres badly damaged. 400,000 people in shelters.

[Slide 15 : Beyond people]
Title: Beyond people
Description: 124,498 birds died. 10,305 sheep and goats. 5,428 cows. 3,191 pigs. US$3.1 million in losses, in places where families live off their animals.

[Slide 16 : Beyond people]
Title: Beyond people
Description: 1,345 km of high-voltage power lines damaged. 10,216 km of local power lines. 3,990 transformers. 30 substations. And the damage keeps spreading. Families who lose their animals and their power feel it long after the water is gone.

[Slide 17 : Who lives and who dies]
Title: Who lives and who dies
Description: This happens all over the world. Since 2016, Africa has had 27% of recorded flood deaths, and Europe 1.5%. But in money, it's the other way round. Europe has 20.2% of the damage and Africa 2.9%. Floods cost the most money in rich countries, but they cost the most lives in poor ones.

[Slide 21 : We know what works]
Title: We know what works
Description: “Disaster-related mortality is at least six times lower in countries with good early-warning systems in place.” (António Guterres, UN Secretary-General) We already have the technology, from volunteers in Bangladesh to river sensors in Malawi. But about 90 countries still haven't reported having a warning system for multiple hazards.

[Slide 22 : But where it matters most, the sensors are missing]
Title: But where it matters most, the sensors are missing
Description: The poorest countries and small island nations provide only 9% of the surface data the WMO asks for. In Africa, the number of weather balloon reports reaching global forecast models halved between 2015 and 2020. Stations get installed and then stop working, because nobody pays to keep them running.

[Slide 23 : We can afford to fix it]
Title: We can afford to fix it
Description: What's missing is the money to build these instruments and keep them working. The poorest countries can't pay for it alone. The UN fund for their missing weather data is asking for US$400 million. The world spends that on its militaries in just over an hour. Every dollar spent on this brings back more than 25.

[Slide 24 : Yet the world is pulling back]
Title: Yet the world is pulling back
Description: The US has been the biggest supporter of this system for a long time, but it is now pulling back. It is launching fewer weather balloons, it paused its famine warning system, it left 66 international organisations, and there are proposals to cut NOAA's budget by a quarter. In Ukraine, the war has destroyed or cut off a quarter of the weather network. Europe's weather satellite agency and the Arctic Council have stopped working with Russia.

[Slide 25 : There can be no gaps in our weather machine]
Title: There can be no gaps in our weather machine
Description: Climate change threatens every country's security, and it hurts most where there are the fewest sensors. The world's highest court has called it "an existential problem of planetary proportions". During the Cold War, rival countries built this system together. This threat is bigger. Every forecast on Earth depends on the same system. No country can see a storm coming on its own.
